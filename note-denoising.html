Denoising, scores, and posterior uncertainty

Statistical inference · Expository derivation

Under Gaussian noise, the posterior mean and covariance follow from the first two derivatives of the noisy-data log density. The derivation also explains why accurate denoising is not, by itself, a guarantee of accurate uncertainty.

The task is to recover both the minimum mean-square error estimate m(y)=E[X∣Y=y]m(y)=\mathbb E[X\mid Y=y]m(y)=E[X∣Y=y] and the remaining uncertainty. 2. Recover the posterior mean from the score

Differentiating the Gaussian kernel gives

The gradient of the log density is the score. The correction to the observation is thus determined by how probability mass changes locally in observation space. This is an exact identity at the given noise level, not a small-noise approximation.

4. Connect denoising error to score error

Thus learning to denoise with squared error also estimates the score of the noisy distribution. No score values need to be supplied as training targets: clean/noisy pairs suffice for the denoising objective. Vincent (2011) develops the connection between denoising and score matching. The identity here specifies its population meaning for additive Gaussian noise.

Application and boundary of the result

For sensor measurements or images corrupted by additive Gaussian noise, these identities connect three tasks: signal recovery, score estimation, and uncertainty quantification. The Gaussian example gives an exact linear baseline; the two-point example shows why a nonlinear response can be necessary even in one dimension.

The covariance identity is exact for the true density. Differentiating an approximate learned score can produce an indefinite matrix or a poor uncertainty estimate. Small score error in mean square does not control its derivatives, so denoising accuracy alone does not certify posterior covariance. Likewise, changing the noise model or inserting this denoiser into a general inverse problem requires a new argument.
