Puzzles

A small collection of problems about information, prediction, and uncertainty. These are exercises in established mathematics, with hints and worked solutions collected below.

1. How many queries buy confidence?

Inference · Minimax risk

A deterministic binary oracle accepts one of N ≥ 2 inputs. It is either clean, returning zero everywhere, or marked, returning one at exactly one unknown input.

You may query at most q distinct inputs, where 0 ≤ q ≤ N, then declare “clean” or “marked.” Both your queries and your decision may be randomized and adaptive.

What is the smallest achievable worst-case probability of error, taking the worst case over the clean oracle and all N possible marked oracles?

Hint

Follow the all-zero transcript. Missing the marked input does not necessarily mean you must declare “clean.”

Solution

The optimal risk is

r* = (N − q) / (2N − q).

To attain it, sample a uniformly random set of q inputs. If any answer is one, report “marked.” Otherwise, report “marked” with probability r*.

The clean oracle is misclassified with probability r*. For every marked oracle, the marked input is missed with probability (N − q)/N. The error probability is therefore

[(N − q) / N] (1 − r*) = r*.

For the lower bound, consider any randomized strategy. Let α be its probability of reporting “marked” on the clean oracle, and include all randomness, including its final decision, in a single random seed.

For each seed on which it reports “clean,” at least N − q marked inputs lie outside its clean-oracle query set. Coupling a marked run to the same seed gives identical zero answers, adaptive queries, and final decision whenever the marked input is outside that set. Averaging over seeds and uniformly over marked inputs therefore gives an error of at least [(N − q)/N](1 − α).

The worst-case error is consequently at least the larger of α and [(N − q)/N](1 − α). Balancing these two terms gives α = r* and proves optimality.

Except when every input can be queried, an optimal minimax detector deliberately accepts some false positives.

2. Can two calibrated forecasts spoil each other?

Probability · Calibration

Let Y ∈ {0, 1} be an outcome and P, Q ∈ [0, 1] two probabilistic forecasts for it. Both are perfectly calibrated:

E[Y | P] = P,   E[Y | Q] = Q.

For a fixed weight 0 < λ < 1, form the average R = λP + (1 − λ)Q.

Without any independence assumptions, when is R also perfectly calibrated?

Hint

Calibration implies E[YP] = E[P2]. Compare E[YR] with E[R2].

Solution

The average is calibrated if and only if P = Q almost surely.

Individual calibration gives

E[YR] = λE[P2] + (1 − λ)E[Q2].

Expanding R2 and subtracting yields

E[R(Y − R)] = λ(1 − λ) E[(P − Q)2].

If R is calibrated, conditioning on R makes the left-hand side zero. Since λ(1 − λ) > 0, this forces P = Q almost surely. Conversely, if the forecasts agree almost surely, their average equals either calibrated forecast.

For example, take a fair binary outcome, the perfect forecast P = Y, and the constant forecast Q = 1/2. Both are calibrated. Their equal-weight average predicts 1/4 when the outcome is always zero, and 3/4 when it is always one.

Exact calibration is not preserved by ordinary weighted averaging.

3. A test you can watch forever

Sequential inference · Martingales

Independent fair coin flips give Xt ∈ {−1, +1}. Begin with wealth W0 = 1. Before each flip, choose ft ∈ [−1, 1] using only previous flips, then update

Wt = Wt−1(1 + ftXt).

You declare an alarm whenever wealth reaches or exceeds 100. There is no time limit, and your betting strategy may change after every observation.

What is the largest possible probability of ever declaring an alarm?

Hint

Stop at the alarm or at a fixed finite horizon, whichever comes first. For sharpness, try betting one unit while you can.

Solution

The exact answer is 1/100.

Wealth is nonnegative, and E[Wt | X1, …, Xt−1] = Wt−1, so it is a martingale. Let τ be the first alarm time. For every finite n, bounded stopping gives

1 = E[Wmin(τ,n)] ≥ 100 Pr(τ ≤ n).

Letting n increase proves Pr(τ < ∞) ≤ 1/100.

To attain the bound, whenever wealth is an integer j ∈ {1, …, 99}, choose ft = 1/j. Wealth moves to j + 1 or j − 1 with equal probability. Stop betting at zero or 100.

This random walk reaches a boundary almost surely: a block of 100 consecutive positive flips forces absorption, and independent blocks eventually contain such a run. Its probability hj of reaching 100 first satisfies

h0 = 0,   h100 = 1,
hj = (hj−1 + hj+1) / 2.

Thus hj = j/100, giving an alarm probability of exactly 1/100 from initial wealth one.

The upper bound is an instance of Ville's inequality; the sharpness construction is the classical gambler's ruin problem. For a modern treatment of the inequality and its role in sequential inference, see Howard et al., Time-uniform Chernoff bounds via nonnegative supermartingales.
