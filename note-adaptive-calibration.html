Sensor calibration with adaptive measurements

Applied probability · Worked analysis

A calibration experiment chooses its next excitation from earlier measurements. This changes the statistical problem: the final design matrix depends on past noise. The derivation keeps that dependence explicit, constructs a valid confidence ellipsoid, and uses its geometry to expose a confounded experiment.

The calibration problemMatrix integral and guaranteeA confounded design

1. Calibrate a sensor while choosing its next input

A sensor's response depends on an unknown gain, cross-talk coefficients, and possibly known basis functions of its operating conditions. Calibration is expensive, so the next excitation is chosen using the measurements already collected. We want both an estimate of the coefficients and an uncertainty bound valid for this adaptive experiment.

Let the feature vector xt∈Rdx_t\in\mathbb R^dxt​∈Rd be known before observing

yt=xt⊤θ∗+ηt.y_t=x_t^\top\theta_*+\eta_t.yt​=xt⊤​θ∗​+ηt​.

Use a filtration (Ft)(\mathcal F_t)(Ft​) containing the observations through time ttt. The design xtx_txt​ is Ft−1\mathcal F_{t-1}Ft−1​-measurable; the coefficient vector is fixed and satisfies ∥θ∗∥2≤S\|\theta_*\|_2\le S∥θ∗​∥2​≤S. For known R>0R>0R>0, assume conditional sub-Gaussian noise:

E[eαηt∣Ft−1]≤eα2R2/2for every α∈R.\mathbb E[e^{\alpha\eta_t}\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]\le e^{\alpha^2R^2/2}\quad\text{for every }\alpha\in\mathbb R.E[eαηt​∣Ft−1​]≤eα2R2/2for every α∈R.

Independent Gaussian noise is one case, but is not required. A variance bound alone is insufficient. Fix a regularization parameter λ>0\lambda>0λ>0 and a failure probability δ∈(0,1)\delta\in(0,1)δ∈(0,1) before evaluation.

The target is an ellipsoid containing the entire coefficient vector at every time, so that a future operating condition may itself be selected from the data. This derives the self-normalized confidence construction of Abbasi-Yadkori, Pál and Szepesvári (2011), Theorems 1–2, then uses its geometry to compare identifiable and confounded calibration designs.

2. Why conditioning on the finished design is unsafe

Ordinary fixed-design calculations treat the entire design matrix as fixed independently of the noise. An adaptive design can encode earlier errors. In one dimension, take θ∗=0\theta_*=0θ∗​=0, x1=1x_1=1x1​=1, and choose x2=1{y1≥0}x_2=\mathbf1_{\{y_1\ge0\}}x2​=1{y1​≥0}​. With independent standard Gaussian noise, observing x2=1x_2=1x2​=1 reveals that the first noise was nonnegative, and

E[η1∣x2=1]=2/π≠0.\mathbb E[\eta_1\mid x_2=1]=\sqrt{2/\pi}\ne0.E[η1​∣x2​=1]=2/π​=0.

This follows by integrating 2z e−z2/2/2π2z\,e^{-z^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}2ze−z2/2/2π​ over the positive half-line. Conditioning on the completed design therefore does not preserve the centered Gaussian law one might try to use. The remedy is to condition only on the information available before each new noise term.

Define the regularized information matrix, accumulated noise, and ridge estimate:

Gt=λI+∑s=1txsxs⊤,zt=∑s=1txsηs,G_t=\lambda I+\sum_{s=1}^{t}x_sx_s^\top,\qquad z_t=\sum_{s=1}^{t}x_s\eta_s,Gt​=λI+s=1∑t​xs​xs⊤​,zt​=s=1∑t​xs​ηs​,

The central difficulty is that GtG_tGt​ and ztz_tzt​ are dependent. A valid analysis must control their relationship without freezing the random design after the fact.

5. Obtain one event valid at every time

Let τ\tauτ be the first time the mixture reaches 1/δ1/\delta1/δ. For a finite horizon NNN, bounded optional stopping and nonnegativity give

Pr⁡(τ≤N)δ≤E[Mτ∧N]≤1.\frac{\Pr(\tau\le N)}{\delta}\le\mathbb E[M_{\tau\wedge N}]\le1.δPr(τ≤N)​≤E[Mτ∧N​]≤1.

∥zt∥Gt−1≤Rlog⁡det⁡Gtλd+2log⁡(1/δ).\|z_t\|_{G_t^{-1}}\le R\sqrt{\log\frac{\det G_t}{\lambda^d}+2\log(1/\delta)}.∥zt​∥Gt−1​​≤RlogλddetGt​​+2log(1/δ)​.

6. Turn the ellipsoid into a design criterion

For any operating condition xxx, Cauchy–Schwarz in the matrix norm gives

∣x⊤(θ^t−θ∗)∣≤βtx⊤Gt−1x.|x^\top(\widehat\theta_t-\theta_*)|\le\beta_t\sqrt{x^\top G_t^{-1}x}.∣x⊤(θt​−θ∗​)∣≤βt​x⊤Gt−1​x​.

This bounds the conditional mean response, not an individual future noisy measurement. Because it follows from a single vector event, it holds for all xxx at once, including an operating condition selected after seeing the data.

The geometry also quantifies what a new probe contributes. Let ℓt=xt⊤Gt−1−1xt\ell_t=x_t^\top G_{t-1}^{-1}x_tℓt​=xt⊤​Gt−1−1​xt​. Factor the matrix update as

Gt=Gt−11/2(I+vv⊤)Gt−11/2,v=Gt−1−1/2xt.G_t=G_{t-1}^{1/2}(I+vv^\top)G_{t-1}^{1/2},\qquad v=G_{t-1}^{-1/2}x_t.Gt​=Gt−11/2​(I+vv⊤)Gt−11/2​,v=Gt−1−1/2​xt​.

The rank-one matrix vv⊤vv^\topvv⊤ has one eigenvalue ∥v∥2=ℓt\|v\|^2=\ell_t∥v∥2=ℓt​ and all others zero. Taking determinants gives

If ∥xs∥2≤L\|x_s\|_2\le L∥xs​∥2​≤L, the trace is at most dλ+tL2d\lambda+tL^2dλ+tL2. Applying the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality to the positive eigenvalues yields

log⁡det⁡Gtλd≤dlog⁡ ⁣(1+tL2dλ).\log\frac{\det G_t}{\lambda^d}\le d\log\!\left(1+\frac{tL^2}{d\lambda}\right).logλddetGt​​≤dlog(1+dλtL2​).

These steps explain why repeatedly measuring an already well-measured direction has diminishing geometric value. Among currently feasible probes, maximizing leverage maximizes the next log-determinant increment. It need not minimize every directional prediction error, or be globally optimal over a constrained sequence of future experiments.

7. Twenty measurements can still leave a gain contrast unidentified

Consider two coefficients and probes x=(1,z)⊤x=(1,z)^\topx=(1,z)⊤ with z∈{−1,1}z\in\{-1,1\}z∈{−1,1}. Use λ=1\lambda=1λ=1, R=0.1R=0.1R=0.1, S=1S=1S=1, and δ=0.05\delta=0.05δ=0.05. The repeated design even has a slightly smaller scalar radius, yet a much worse directional guarantee. Reporting a sample count or the scalar radius alone hides the problem. The information matrix identifies which combinations of coefficients the experiment actually measures. These are analytical design calculations, not measured sensor performance.

8. The modeling boundary

The proof permits data-dependent excitation and stopping, but retains a fixed coefficient vector and a valid conditional noise bound. Drift, an omitted nonlinear response, errors in the recorded features, or noise with a history-dependent conditional mean can break those assumptions. Adding more samples does not repair non-identifiability when every probe lies in the same subspace.

The numerical checks verify matrix square completion, the determinant update, and both calibration designs. The confidence result itself comes from the supermartingale proof, not from those finite checks. Its link to experiment design is the practical payoff: the same matrix that normalizes the noise records what the experiment has learned and where it remains uncertain.
